## The one-minute version (the settings, listed)

If you only read one section, read this one:

- **Landscape orientation**, not vertical.
- **4K at 30fps**, if your phone supports it. 60fps only if you specifically want slow motion later.
- **Lock exposure and focus** before kickoff: tap and hold on the field, not the players.
- **Stand as high as you're allowed**, as close to the halfway line as the space permits.
- **Shoot wider than feels right.** You can crop in later; you cannot add back detail that was never captured.
- **Full battery, a spare if you have one, and enough storage for the whole game**, checked before you leave the house, not at halftime.

Everything below explains why, and what happens if you skip one.

## Why wide beats zoomed: cropping needs pixels to work with

The instinct on the sideline is to zoom in on your kid so they fill the frame. It feels like the safe choice in the moment, and it is almost always the wrong one.

A zoomed shot only works while your player stays inside it. The moment play moves (a break down the wing, a fast transition, a substitution you didn't see coming), the zoomed frame either cuts them off or you're panning and losing focus trying to keep up. A wide shot has no such failure mode: your kid can be anywhere on the field and the footage still has them in it, just small.

The reason this matters for editing specifically: cropping in during editing needs pixels to crop *from*. A wide 4K frame has enough resolution that cropping down to a tight, kid-sized frame in post still leaves a clean, sharp image. A frame that was already zoomed on a phone camera has no spare resolution to give. Crop it further and you're just enlarging blur. Wide-and-steady footage can always be tightened afterwards. Tight-and-shaky footage cannot be rescued.

## Resolution and frame rate: what 4K actually buys you

4K resolution is roughly four times the pixel count of 1080p. On a phone screen the difference is barely visible, which is why it's easy to assume it doesn't matter. It matters enormously once you plan to crop.

Think of it as a resolution budget. If you film at 4K and later crop to a tight frame around your player (the kind of crop a wide sideline shot needs), you're spending some of that budget on the crop and still ending up with a sharp, usable 1080p (or better) result. Film at 1080p to begin with and there's no budget left to spend; a crop just produces a soft, pixelated result.

Frame rate is a separate decision. 30fps is the right default for almost every youth sports recording: it looks natural and keeps file sizes manageable. 60fps is worth the extra storage only if you specifically want smooth slow-motion replays of a particular play (a diving save, a big hit, a finish at the line); shoot the whole game at 60fps only if storage and battery genuinely aren't a constraint, since it roughly doubles both.

## Where to stand: height beats proximity

Given the choice between standing closer to the field or standing higher above it, choose height. An elevated angle (team bleachers, a hill, the top row of a stand) looks down onto the field rather than across it, which means players don't visually overlap each other as much and your kid stays identifiable even in a crowd near the ball.

Proximity has a lower ceiling than people expect. Standing right on the touchline puts you level with the play, which means the players nearest you block the players further away, and a fast break can carry the whole play out of your sightline in seconds. A slightly further but higher position almost always produces more usable footage than a close but flat one.

If height isn't available, the next best thing is a position as close to the halfway line as the rules and space allow, since that minimizes how far the play travels away from your line of sight in either direction.

## Locking exposure and focus so the frame stops pulsing

Phone cameras adjust exposure and focus continuously by default, which is exactly wrong for a moving outdoor scene. Every time a player, a shadow, or a patch of sky crosses the frame, the camera re-meters and the whole image visibly brightens or dims: the "pulsing" effect that makes handheld sports footage look amateurish even when the framing is good.

Before kickoff, tap and hold on a mid-toned part of the field (grass or turf, not a bright sky or a dark shadow) until your phone's camera app locks exposure and focus. Most phones show an "AE/AF LOCK" indicator when this happens. Re-lock at halftime or if you move to a different sideline, since lighting conditions change with the sun's position and a new spot needs a fresh lock.

This single habit removes the single most common visible flaw in parent-filmed sideline footage, and it costs nothing: no extra gear, no extra time, just a two-second tap before the whistle.

## Landscape vs vertical, and why landscape wins even for Instagram

It's tempting to film vertically because that's how a phone naturally sits in your hand, and because vertical is the native format for Instagram Stories and TikTok. Film landscape anyway, for one reason: a landscape source can always be cropped to a vertical export later, and a vertical source can never be uncropped to landscape.

If the eventual use for a clip is a recruiting reel, a wide cut needs the full landscape frame: a field sport in particular needs the horizontal space to show shape, spacing, and the run of play, none of which fits in a vertical crop. Film landscape once, and both a landscape recruiting cut and a vertical social cut can come from the same footage. Film vertical once, and the recruiting cut is gone before you've even started editing.

## Storage and battery maths for a full game

A full youth sports game runs 60 to 100 minutes of clock time before stoppages, substitutions, and halftime are added in. Plan for closer to two hours of camera-on time if you intend to keep rolling through breaks rather than stopping and restarting.

4K30 video runs at roughly 350-400MB per minute on most modern phones (the exact rate varies by device and codec), which puts a two-hour recording in the 40-50GB range. Check your phone's free storage before you leave the house, not when a "storage full" warning interrupts a play you needed. If storage is tight, recording several shorter clips (halves, or quarters) rather than one continuous file is a reasonable trade: you lose nothing except the ability to scrub across a stoppage, which you weren't going to use anyway.

Battery drains faster while actively recording video than in almost any other phone use, especially with the screen on and GPS active. A phone at 100% charge, in airplane mode with GPS off, screen brightness low, can usually make it through a full game, but a portable battery pack is cheap insurance, and worth keeping in the bag regardless.

## Mounts, tripods, and going hands-free

Handheld footage, even from someone trying to hold still, has a visible low-frequency wobble that reads as unprofessional and is tiring to watch for more than a few minutes. A tripod removes it completely and costs very little: a basic phone tripod is inexpensive and takes seconds to set up pitch-side.

A tripod also frees you to actually watch the game, which matters more than it sounds: filming and parenting from the sideline at the same time is genuinely hard, and a mounted phone means you can cheer, coach from the sideline, or just watch, while the camera keeps rolling regardless. If a tripod isn't practical for your venue, bracing your elbows against your body and against a fixed object (a fence, a railing, your knee if seated) removes most of the wobble even without one.

## Common mistakes that make footage unusable

In order of how often they ruin an otherwise good recording:

- **Stopping and restarting during play.** Every stop is a moment you might have missed something; let the camera run continuously through a half rather than stop-starting for each play.
- **Panning too fast.** A fast pan blurs every frame it passes through into unusable motion blur, even though it looks fine to your eye in the moment. Pan slower than feels natural, or don't pan at all and rely on the wide shot to keep the player in frame.
- **Filming through a fence or netting** at a distance where it's in sharp focus. Standing further back, or filming through a gap, keeps the fence out of focus enough to become invisible.
- **Forgetting to re-lock exposure after moving.** Covered above, and worth repeating because it's the easiest one to forget mid-game.
- **Recording into direct sun.** Position yourself with the sun behind or to the side of the camera, not the players facing into it toward you. Filming toward the sun silhouettes everyone and blows out the sky.

## What to do with the footage afterwards

Wide, steady, 4K footage is exactly the raw material a highlight reel needs: the job afterwards is finding the moments worth keeping and cropping each one down to a tight, watchable frame around your player. Doing that by hand in a general-purpose video editor is possible but slow: scrubbing a full game to find plays, then manually keyframing a crop for each one, easily takes several hours per game.
