# Pilot Twelve Labs before building player tracking

No existing model or service sells the exact capability ReelBallers wants (send any 60-90 minute game, name a player, get back scored time ranges) as a self-serve API. The sports vendors that do per-player highlights (Veo, Trace, Pixellot, Spiideo) lock the feature to their own cameras; the bring-your-own-video consumer apps (PlayerTrac, Vidio.ai, NextPlay, Soccer Pro AI) have no public API and rely on a tap or manual marking; and the general video-understanding APIs return timestamps for a text query but have never been shown reading a jersey number on amateur footage. The closest fit is **Twelve Labs**: its Marengo 3.0 search returns time-stamped, confidence-scored clips for a natural-language query over a whole 90-minute file in one index, its docs claim jersey-number and on-screen-text indexing, and it costs roughly **$4-7 per game** on the legacy pay-as-you-go rates or **$29-99 per month plus storage** under the plan model that takes over in January 2027. **Gemini** is 10-50x cheaper (**$0.06-$2.26 per game**) and equally simple to call from FastAPI, but returns no native score, has a documented record of timestamp drift on long videos, and samples frames too coarsely to read a two-digit number on a wide shot. The only approach demonstrated on phone-shot youth soccer is a **self-hosted tracker the parent seeds by tapping the child** (Roboflow's reelcut, July 2026), which would cost **$0.50-4 of Modal GPU per game** but **15-25 engineering days**. The recommendation is a one-week Twelve Labs pilot on 3-5 hand-labeled games (under $50 total) using jersey-number-plus-kit-color queries and a tap-to-crop composed query, with a hard kill criterion; if recall on parent-labeled moments is unacceptable, fall back to the tap-to-track pipeline on Modal for identity and use Gemini or Qwen3-VL only to score the candidate windows. Every score from every candidate is a ranking signal, not a probability; calibration will have to come from ReelBallers' own accept/reject gestures.

## No vendor sells per-player moments for arbitrary footage, and identity is the gap

The youth-soccer market splits into five segments, and none of them exposes a metered "send us a game, get player X's clips" endpoint. The camera-locked systems are the only ones that identify a player by jersey number without a tap: Veo Player Moments "is available for Football/Soccer only," applies only to recordings made after buying the Analytics 2 add-on, and Analytics 2 "requires Veo Editor and a Veo Cam" ([Veo support](https://support.veo.com/hc/en-us/articles/27698176364817-Overview-of-Veo-Player-Moments-How-to-track-and-follow-players-in-your-Veo-recordings)). Trace PlayerFocus ($180-300 per year with the camera folded in) has the parent enter kit colors and a jersey number and then "combines that data with hundreds of other signals," but only on Trace-camera games and with no API mentioned anywhere on its pricing or help pages ([Trace how it works](https://traceup.com/how-it-works); [Trace pricing](https://traceup.com/pricing)). Pixellot's partner API is an operations API for creating events and receiving clip webhooks, marked proprietary and confidential, with no per-player filtering documented ([Pixellot API docs](https://docs.pixellot.tv/portal/en/kb/articles/api-integration-events-and-clubs)); the one B2B2C player-reel arrangement found, SportsEngine Play's "Gamecast" module built on Pixellot, is a platform partnership that does not state its identification method or parent pricing ([Youth Sports Business Report](https://youthsportsbusinessreport.com/?p=19789)). Veo acknowledges a partner API but is "accepting new partners on a limited basis," and all its player features are documented as Veo-Cam-only ([Veo partner page](https://www.veo.co/en-us/become-a-partner)).

The bring-your-own-video products that do isolate one player all push identity onto the user. PlayerTrac accepts footage "from any source" and has the user click the player in one frame; it publishes no price and its homepage would not render ([PlayerTrac blog](https://playertrac.ai/blog/best-ai-soccer-player-tracking-software/)). Vidio.ai's "Click once to lock on" player highlighting is the only one with a published per-game-equivalent price: "AI edit for player-specific cuts" costs 0.1 credits per second, which for a 90-minute game is 540 credits, about **$54 on the $40/month Business plan or $43 on the $160/month Studio plan**, and API access is by request form with no public terms ([Vidio pricing](https://www.vidio.ai/pricing); [Vidio developer page](https://www.vidio.ai/developer-api/soccer-highlight-video-api)). NextPlay explicitly "does not track players or read jersey numbers"; the parent taps "Mark athlete" on each detected play, a full game takes "over an hour" to analyze, and the Athlete plan is capped at 10 full games a month for $14.99 ([NextPlay](https://nextplayhighlights.com/)). Soccer Pro AI has the user drop a "Numbered Pin" and specify kit color for $9.99 a month ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id6758618341)); Hylytr claims "Auto-track any player" and "95% Detection Accuracy" with no methodology ([Hylytr](https://hylytr.com/platform/)). XbotGo Pro Soccer is the cheapest published per-match AI price in the segment at **$15 per match** (regular $23.99), but it is team-level highlights and a tactical report, and the launch describes only XbotGo-camera footage ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/xbotgo-launches-pro-soccer-bringing-professional-ai-tactical-analysis-and-multi-tier-highlights-to-grassroots-football-302864734.html)). Enterprise players (WSC Sports, Magnifi, Second Spectrum, SkillCorner) are sales-led with no youth tier.

Two findings from this segment should shape expectations for any route. First, even the best camera-locked jersey OCR degrades visibly on youth kits: Veo documents that numbers 1-50 "typically perform better," 76-99 are least accurate, and duplicate numbers on a team "significantly affect your results" ([Veo support](https://support.veo.com/hc/en-us/articles/49272029444241-How-to-get-the-best-results-with-Veo-Analytics-2)), and a commenter on Veo's idea board reported numbers wrong 40-50 percent of the time (anecdotal) ([Veo Ideas](https://veo.nolt.io/1922)). Second, **no consumer soccer product outputs a per-moment importance score**; Veo's Player Moments selects a playlist "based on a combination of rules" with user-adjustable closeness-to-ball and dwell time, and "ratings or scores are not mentioned" ([Veo updates](https://updates.veo.co/announcements/veo-player-moments)). Scores exist only in broadcast tooling such as Reely's "hype score" from announcer tone and scoreboard changes ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/reely-instant-highlights-ai/)). The industry has converged on event tags plus proximity rules rather than a learned score, which means ReelBallers would be among the first to ship a ranked list to parents regardless of route.

## Twelve Labs is the one API built for "query to scored timestamps"; Gemini is the cheap alternative without scores

Among dedicated video-understanding platforms, Twelve Labs is the only one whose product is literally the workflow in the question. Its Search API returns "time-stamped, confidence-scored moments rather than whole videos," the index covers "visual content, audio, speech (ASR), and on-screen text," Marengo processes "4 hours of continuous video and audio ... in a single call," and the Pegasus product page says it "reads on-screen text, jersey numbers, whiteboards, and receipts" with a 2-hour limit per Analyze request ([Twelve Labs llms-full.txt](https://www.twelvelabs.io/llms-full.txt)). The sports evidence is vendor-produced but specific: the Marengo 3.0 launch post benchmarks a soccer query describing "a red player dribbling toward the goal, then blue player 11 holding and dribbling the ball" and ranks the correct video first ahead of Amazon Nova and Google Vertex, reports 79.4 mAP on SoccerNet-Action versus Nova's 23.0 and Vertex's 21.5, and includes a composed basketball query combining an image of a specific player with the text "scored a jump shot" ([Twelve Labs blog: Marengo 3.0](https://www.twelvelabs.io/blog/marengo-3-0)). That composed image-plus-text query is the hook for a tap UI: the user taps the child, the app crops that frame, and the crop becomes the image half of the query.

The evidence has three soft spots that should be named plainly. The "blue player 11" result is a retrieval ranking among videos, not a per-moment accuracy figure, and the post makes "no jersey-number or sports-specific text recognition claims" beyond general OCR benchmarks ([Twelve Labs blog: Marengo 3.0](https://www.twelvelabs.io/blog/marengo-3-0)). Twelve Labs' own showcased sports apps identify athletes by name ("Glenn Maxwell") or as "person-1 / person-2"; none uses a jersey number ([Twelve Labs docs: sports](https://docs.twelvelabs.io/docs/sports)). And the official Search API reference returned 404 on 2026-10-31, so per-result field names and the definition of the confidence buckets are unconfirmed from the primary source; what is known is that results carry a relevance score and that a community app sorts by it with "a confidence adjustment of 0.5" ([Twelve Labs docs: sports](https://docs.twelvelabs.io/docs/sports)). All of its sports benchmarks are on broadcast footage. Nothing has been published on phone or Veo footage from any API vendor.

Gemini is the second-least-engineering path and by far the cheapest. All Gemini models accept video natively at a default 1 frame per second, about 100 tokens per second at low media resolution and about 300 at high, with a 1M-token window that holds "up to 3 hours at low media resolution, or up to 1 hour at high," timestamps requested in MM:SS, and `start_offset`/`end_offset` clipping so one uploaded file can be queried in windows ([Gemini video understanding docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/video-understanding)). Three things count against it for this use case. It returns no score field; any 0-1 confidence is whatever the LLM writes into a JSON schema, and the one academic pipeline that used such a field "does not describe calibration or how the score is computed" ([arXiv 2610.37583](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.37583)). Its long-video timestamps have a forum record of unreliability: on 2.5 Pro, timestamps "did not match content and some ran past the video's length" with splitting into short segments the suggested workaround ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-2-5-pro-severe-timestamp-timecode-jumping-issues-in-video-transcription-need-workarounds/87242)), and on 3 Flash and 3.1 Pro a progressive drift reaching about 16 seconds by the end of a file ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/bug-gemini-3-flash-and-3-1-pro-progressive-timestamp-drift-in-audio-transcription/129501)). And the cheap default resolution is 70 tokens per frame, with `high` (280 tokens) recommended only "for reading dense text or small details" ([Gemini media resolution docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/media-resolution)); a two-digit number on a Veo wide shot is exactly such a small detail, so readable identity means 4x the cost and a 1-hour cap that forces at least two calls. Google's agentic video mode claims "up to 88%" fewer tokens and "sub-second moment retrieval," but these are marketing claims on unnamed benchmarks, the docs warn broad prompts can use more tokens than static mode, and "every moment involving #7 across 90 minutes" is a broad prompt ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-agentic-video-in-gemini/)). Gemini was also the weakest in its Sports Competition category on Video-MME, with performance declining as duration grows ([Video-MME paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.21075)).

The rest of the API field is either a building-block kit or a dead end. Azure Content Understanding's `prebuilt-videoSearch` auto-segments into "short clips of 15-30 seconds on average," extracts a custom field schema per segment, and "generates confidence scores" at roughly $3.33 per hour on GPT-5.2 ([MS Learn pricing explainer](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/content-understanding/pricing-explainer)); it is a credible third option but has no sports evidence and its segments are coarse for a soccer play. Google Video Intelligence outputs labels, shots, object tracks and text boxes with "no semantic video search," so linking a jersey OCR hit to a person track across 90 minutes is your code ([Mixpeek curated list](https://mixpeek.com/curated-lists/best-video-understanding-platforms)). Amazon Rekognition discontinued People Pathing on October 31, 2025, so it cannot even return anonymous player tracks anymore ([AWS Rekognition docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/rekognition/latest/dg/persons.html)). Memories.ai, Cloudglue and Mixpeek return embedding-similarity scores, with face libraries as their only identity mechanism, which is near-useless on sideline footage of youth players at field distance. OpenAI and Claude have no video input at all; GPT-6 Astra's model page lists text and image only ([OpenAI GPT-6 Astra](https://developers.openai.com/docs/models/gpt-6-astra)), and the Claude vision page covers images only with 600 per request ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/docs/build-with-claude/vision)), so a game becomes 2,700-5,400 frames across 4-85 calls at $1.3-58 for jersey-legible detail. The single best demonstrated LLM result on soccer, GPT-5.5 reaching F1@5s of 72.02 for event spotting, used 15-second broadcast clips with Whisper commentary transcripts and "does not describe any attempt at player identification" ([arXiv 2610.37583](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.37583)); youth games have no commentary, and frames alone scored 63.57.

On the probability question the answer is uniform: **nobody returns a calibrated probability that a moment involves a given player.** Twelve Labs returns retrieval relevance with bucketed confidence; Mixpeek and Memories.ai return similarity scores; Azure returns field-level confidence; Gemini and Pegasus return whatever the LLM writes. Google's own Vertex video cookbook recommends putting a "reasoning" field before the "score" field in the JSON schema for consistent scores ([Vertex AI video prompting recipe](https://googlecloudplatform.github.io/applied-ai-engineering-samples/genai-on-vertex-ai/gemini/prompting_recipes/multimodal/multimodal_prompting_video/)), which is the right prompt pattern but still yields an ordinal self-assessment. Any route therefore needs the same last mile: threshold empirically on a few labeled games and fit a mapping from stated score to observed precision using user confirmations.

File size is a shared integration detail that Modal already solves. The Gemini Files API page states 2 GB per file with automatic deletion after 48 hours ([Gemini Files API docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/files)), while the video page says 20 GB on paid, an internal inconsistency; budgeting for 2 GB is safe. Since Gemini samples at 1 fps anyway, a 1-2 Mbps re-encode loses little the model would use, and the same transcode shrinks a Twelve Labs upload to a fraction of the storage cap. The 48-hour retention also means re-querying a game for a second sibling later requires a re-upload, so the transcoded file should stay in R2.

## Self-hosting on Modal is the only path demonstrated on phone footage, at 15-25 engineering days

The one open project aimed squarely at this use case is Roboflow's reelcut (published July 22, 2026): an RF-DETR-small five-class detector plus BoT-SORT with camera-motion compensation turned off, an RF-DETR-nano digit detector reading only the torso region with 3-second enrollment and majority voting, kit color as a secondary cue, and a FastAPI web app where "the parent clicks the child and types number + kit color" ([Roboflow reelcut post](https://roboflow.com/blog/make-automatic-highlight-reels-from-soccer-games)). It is a self-described prototype and its reported failure modes are instructive: the scoreboard was misread as "#2" 31 times, two adjacent players produced merged numbers like "71," CMC caused 21 identity switches versus 5 without on phone footage, CLIP appearance embeddings were abandoned because every player scored 0.88-0.95 similarity to the target, goals were scored heuristically by ball-in-goal-box persistence, and a full game took about an hour on Roboflow batch processing, "largely queueing and provisioning" ([Roboflow reelcut post](https://roboflow.com/blog/make-automatic-highlight-reels-from-soccer-games)). The author reports counts, not precision and recall. Everything else on GitHub (abdullahtarek/football_analysis, roboflow/sports, Tryolabs, PlayerTV) is broadcast tracking or possession demo code; roboflow/sports lists jersey reading and re-ID as unsolved challenges ([roboflow/sports](https://github.com/roboflow/sports)).

The academic numbers that make this stack look mature are all broadcast numbers and should be discounted for a static wide shot where players are 30-60 pixels tall. SoccerNet tracking tops out at 75.61 HOTA end-to-end ([sn-tracking](https://github.com/SoccerNet/sn-tracking)) and GTA post-processing lifts several trackers to 83.11 ([GTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.08216v1)), but no HOTA has been published on phone or Veo footage, and the one amateur fixed-camera dataset, SoccerTrack v2, "ships no baselines yet" ([SoccerTrack v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.01802v1)). Tracklet-level jersey accuracy on SoccerNet crops reaches 92.85% for the 2023 challenge winner ([sn-jersey](https://github.com/SoccerNet/sn-jersey)) and Grad's single-stage ViT gives a usable Dirichlet uncertainty per read at 85-88% ([Grad project page](https://lukaszgrad.github.io/jnr)), yet generic OCR collapses to 30.6% on Eliteserien footage in PlayerTV, whose authors call OCR "the main bottleneck" ([PlayerTV](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16076v1)). The 2026 Spiideo Synloc winner needed adaptive tiling of 4K frames to see far players at all ([SoccerNet 2026 results](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.07320v1)), which quadruples detector cost. Action spotting is the cheap part: E2E-Spot runs at 0.3-0.6 ms per frame at 224p ([E2E-Spot](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.10213)) and T-DEED reached 73.39 mAP@1 on 12 ball-action classes ([Action spotting survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01304)), but no spotter has been evaluated on non-broadcast footage, none reports calibration, and the player-centric variant that also names who acted tops out at 58.94 macro-F1 even on broadcast ([SoccerNet 2026 results](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.07320v1)). Open VLMs are a re-ranker, not a detector: Qwen3-VL-32B scores 0.45 zero-shot on 4-choice SoccerNet VQA (random 0.25) and reaches 0.78 only after fine-tuning on 8 H100s ([MSUE](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12106v1)).

The GPU bill is small and Modal is the cheapest serverless option for it: L4 at $0.000222 per second ($0.80 per hour), A10 $1.10, A100-80 GB $2.50, H100 $3.95, billed per second with $30 of monthly credit ([Modal pricing](https://modal.com/pricing)), versus RunPod serverless L4 at $0.75 and H100 at $5.39 ([RunPod pricing](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)). On the researchers' throughput anchors (YOLO11m at 4.7 ms per frame on T4 TensorRT ([YOLO11 docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo11/)); Constructor.Tech's full pipeline at 80 fps on a laptop GPU with DeepStream ([From Broadcast to Minimap](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06357v1))), a detector-plus-tracker pass is 23-45 GPU-minutes, jersey digits 4-8 minutes, spotting 5-14 minutes, so a simple stack is roughly **$0.45-0.90 per game on an L4** and a re-ID stack with GTA **$1.35-2.70**, with tiling for far players adding $0.90-1.80. The cost that matters is engineering: wiring four models end to end on Modal (5-8 days), labeling and fine-tuning the detector and digit model on ReelBallers' own footage (5-10 days), and an evaluation harness on 3-5 hand-labeled games with threshold tuning (3-5 days), for 15-25 days to a first usable version. Licensing shapes the picks: Ultralytics YOLO11/26 is AGPL-3.0 or Enterprise, T-DEED is GPL-3.0, while RF-DETR, YOLOX, Supervision, sn-reid, lRomul's spotter and Qwen3-VL are permissive ([YOLO11 docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo11/); [T-DEED](https://github.com/arturxe2/T-DEED); [lRomul](https://github.com/lRomul/ball-action-spotting); [Qwen3-VL-8B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct)).

This route fits ReelBallers structurally. Modal is already the GPU tier, FFmpeg decode and transcode already run there, R2 already holds the game, and the editor already has the gesture vocabulary (tap on the player, accept or reject a clip) that this pipeline needs as input and as calibration data. The output contract would be a list of (t_start, t_end, event class, spotter score, player-presence confidence) where player presence is the fraction of frames in the window with the target track within some distance of the ball. The biggest risk is compounding three uncalibrated confidences (track identity, jersey vote, spotter score); surfacing them separately and letting the parent confirm clips is the practical calibration loop.

## Recommendation: one week on Twelve Labs with a kill criterion, then tap-to-track on Modal

**First route: Twelve Labs Marengo 3.0 search, piloted before any product code.** It is the only candidate that returns the three things asked for (time ranges, a score, a query that can name a jersey number) from a hosted endpoint with no GPU to operate, and its sports retrieval benchmarks, while vendor-produced and broadcast-only, are the strongest in the field. The pilot is cheap and bounded: pick 3-5 games from the dev fixture account spanning phone and Veo footage, have a parent (or yourself) hand-label every moment involving one player per game, transcode each game to under 2 GB, index it on the Plus plan ($29, holds 6-9 games), and run three query styles per game: "player number 7 in blue shoots / tackles / dribbles / scores," a kit-color-plus-description query with no number, and a composed query using a tap-to-crop image of the player plus action text, the pattern Marengo 3.0's own benchmark demonstrates ([Twelve Labs blog: Marengo 3.0](https://www.twelvelabs.io/blog/marengo-3-0)). Measure recall against the hand labels at a 5-second tolerance and precision in the top 20 results, and confirm the exact score field and bucket semantics from the live API since the reference docs were unreachable. Total spend is under $50. The kill criterion should be set before the pilot: if the number-based query cannot recover roughly half of the labeled moments for a player whose number is in the 1-50 range on the better-lit games, the hosted route cannot identify the player on this footage class and no prompt engineering will fix that, because the limit is legibility of the number in the index. If it passes, production integration is a FastAPI background task that uploads from R2, polls indexing, runs the saved queries, writes the ranked ranges into the clip proposals, and deletes the index entry to free storage; the editor's player pick is a jersey number plus kit color form with an optional tap that crops the reference image.

Gemini belongs in the same pilot as a $2 side experiment on the same transcoded files, not as the first route. Run 3.8 Flash in static mode with 10-minute `start_offset`/`end_offset` windows at high media resolution, a JSON `response_schema` with a reasoning field before a 0-1 confidence, and the same hand labels. Its purpose is to answer whether an LLM can see the player at all on this footage, which decides how useful it is later as a significance scorer; it should not be the identity engine, since it has no native score, the forum record on long-video timestamps is poor, and chunking into windows is mandatory rather than optional.

**Fallback: tap-to-track on Modal for identity, with a model scoring only the candidate windows.** If the Twelve Labs pilot fails its kill criterion, the failure will be identity, and the fallback has to solve identity differently: the parent taps the child in one frame, the app seeds a track from that tap, and the pipeline carries the identity by tracking and re-anchors it with digit votes and kit color, exactly the reelcut recipe ([Roboflow reelcut post](https://roboflow.com/blog/make-automatic-highlight-reels-from-soccer-games)) on permissive components (RF-DETR or YOLOX detector fine-tuned on 300-500 labeled frames of ReelBallers footage, tiled 2x2; BoT-SORT with CMC off plus a GTA post-pass; a digit detector or Grad's ViT with 3-second enrollment and an UNKNOWN fallback; lRomul's MIT spotter or T-DEED for event timestamps). Significance then comes from intersecting "target track near the ball" with spotted events, and the top candidate windows get an optional re-rank by Gemini or Qwen3-VL-8B at a few cents per game, which is where the pilot's Gemini side experiment pays off. Budget 15-25 engineering days and $1-4 of GPU per game, and treat the first 3-5 hand-labeled games from the pilot as the evaluation set so the two routes are measured on identical data.

Two paths are explicitly not recommended. Vidio.ai is the only product with click-to-lock player cuts on arbitrary video and a published price, but $43-54 per game plus a plan fee, an API that exists only as a request form, and undisclosed scoring make it a demo to study, not a supplier. And any route that depends on a generic OCR, face library or CLIP embedding to find the child should be skipped outright; the research is unambiguous that these collapse on same-kit players at field distance.

## Conclusion

The sharpest finding is that the market has quietly split the problem into two halves and only one of them is for sale. Significance (was this a shot, a tackle, a goal) is cheap and well served by hosted models and open spotters; identity (which of the 22 small blobs is your kid) is the whole product, and every vendor who has solved it either owns the camera or asks the parent to tap. A hosted-API bet is therefore really a bet that the jersey number is legible in the indexed frames, which is why the pilot's kill criterion is about recall on numbered players and nothing else. The second finding is that calibrated probabilities do not exist anywhere in this stack, but ReelBallers is unusually well placed to manufacture them: every accept or reject in the editor is a labeled example tied to a gesture, and a logistic map from raw score to observed precision fitted on a few hundred of those will beat anything a vendor ships. The third is that prices are moving faster than a feature cycle, with Twelve Labs' plan switch on January 4, 2027 and Gemini's doubling on December 1 and January 1, so the pilot's cost table should be re-read, not reused, at launch.

Going forward, the choice between routes is less about accuracy ceilings than about who owns the identity step. If Twelve Labs reads numbers on this footage, ReelBallers ships in weeks with an operational footprint of one API key and a storage quota. If it does not, the fallback is heavier but keeps identity inside a tap the parent already understands, runs on infrastructure ReelBallers already operates, and leaves the hosted models in the role where their evidence is strongest: scoring short clips, not finding people.

## Addendum (2026-10-31): all sports, parent clicks the child, two-tier design under $5 per game

Follow-up question: can a sport-agnostic pipeline where the parent clicks their child, a cheap model decides when the child is in frame, and a better model decides whether the play is critical, actually work for under $5 per game? Two further research notes support this section: `click_seeded_tracking.md` and `criticality_scoring_tier.md` in the research_notes folder.

**Verdict: yes on cost (about $0.50 to $1.50 of compute per game), with a real but measurable identity-loss risk in tier 1 and a moderate, sport-dependent ceiling on tier 2.**

**Tier 1, where is the child (sport-agnostic).** Two architectures were costed on Modal (prices read 2026-10-31: L4 $0.80/hr, A100-80GB $2.50/hr, H100 $3.95/hr).

- Click-prompted segmentation trackers (SAM 2.1 / SAM 3 and the long-video variants DAM4SAM, SAMURAI, SAM2Long, SAM3RL) have the best published single-object persistence (SAM 3 LVOSv2 88.5 J&F; SAM3RL LaSOT 78.4) but must run at full frame rate: SAM 3 at stride 6 drops VOTS2026 from 66.4 to 55.5. At 30 fps a 90-minute game is 162,000 frames, which costs $2.85 to $8.52 on an A100 for SAM 2.1-L (compiled vs. independent harness fps) and about $7.35 for SAM 3. Hosted endpoints are worse: fal SAM 3 $50 per game at 30 fps, Meta's Model API about $32. All memory trackers struggle when the object leaves and re-enters the frame (VOT-LT F-scores 0.62 to 0.79; the one football study measured 8.66 percent off-screen re-acquisition), and none has a sports or small-player evaluation. Research and API video limits are 30 s to 8 minutes, so chunking and re-anchoring are the application's job.
- Detect-then-associate (person detector at 5 to 10 fps plus ByteTrack / ARC-SORT / Deep-EIoU with an appearance re-ID model) tolerates frame subsampling, is person-generic so it covers every sport, and is the only architecture where the parent's clicks (initial plus any later re-click) can be applied as identity constraints when stitching tracklets, repairing history without rerunning the tracker. ARC-SORT (Oct 2026, Apache-2.0) reaches HOTA 83.0 on SportsMOT with offline appearance-based merging for leave and re-enter. Cost on an L4 is roughly $0.30 to $0.60 for detector plus tracker, $0.60 to $1.20 with re-ID; tiling for 30 to 80 px players multiplies detector time by about 5x. SAM 2 and SAM 3 expose an occlusion / presence logit, so the self-hosted path can drive an "ask the parent to re-click" prompt; hosted endpoints do not return it.

Recommendation for tier 1: detect-then-associate at 5 to 10 fps on an L4, with the click as a track-id selector and later clicks as stitching constraints, and a confidence-gated re-click prompt in the editor. No measured re-click rate exists for youth footage anywhere; measure it on 3 to 5 real games before freezing the UX.

**Tier 2, is this play critical for the child.** Costs per game for 240 unfiltered 15-second windows at Gemini's documented ~100 tokens per second of video (low media resolution, audio included): 2.5 Flash-Lite $0.06, 3.5 Flash-Lite $0.22, 3.8 Flash $0.46 (doubles 2027-01-01), 3.1 Pro $1.30; Batch API halves these. Claude Haiku 5.5 on 15 sampled frames per window is about $0.22 per game with no audio. A self-hosted Qwen3-VL-8B on Modal is $0.10 to $0.30 per game if kept warm, but has no published video throughput and would need benchmarking. A nearly free audio pre-filter (frozen AST plus GRU, CPU) keeps about 20 to 25 percent of windows on broadcast multi-sport data (SVHighlights, 8 sports: audio-only mAP 41.8 beat the best video model at 37.8), but commentary carried much of that signal, so expect weaker filtering on crowd-and-whistle amateur audio.

Two evidence-based design rules for tier 2. First, pass the tracked child's box as text coordinates, not as a drawn overlay: the only sports-video test (tennis, VideoLLaMA2-7B) found drawn boxes halved accuracy (39.7 to 18.2) while the same boxes as text raised it (to 61.3). Supply team color and attacking direction as text, since team confusion is the likely systematic error. Second, treat the score as a rank, not a probability: use reasoning-then-score JSON on all windows, a listwise pass over the top 10 to 15 (8 clips in one Gemini call is about $0.003), fit Bradley-Terry or Elo, and calibrate per sport from thumbs-up / thumbs-down. The best published "is this moment important" result for a small open VLM on broadcast soccer is MCC 0.50 / accuracy 0.75, and human raters themselves agree only moderately (Krippendorff alpha: soccer 0.70, basketball 0.65, volleyball 0.72, hockey 0.46), which caps what any model can deliver.

**Per-game total:** transcode ~$0.05, tier 1 $0.30 to $1.20 (L4), audio pre-filter pennies, tier 2 $0.06 to $0.50 on Flash-class Gemini. About $0.50 to $1.50 per game, with headroom to use 3.1 Pro on the top candidates and stay under $3. Engineering, not compute, is the real cost: 15 to 25 days to a first usable version, dominated by fine-tuning the person detector on wide-shot footage and building the evaluation harness.

**Buy alternative, sport-agnostic:** Twelve Labs' composed image-plus-text query (crop from the parent's click plus "scores" or "makes a play") is the only hosted product that accepts the click-seeded identity directly, at about $2 per game of storage on the Pro plan at 50 games per month, but it has no amateur-footage evidence and its per-moment confidence buckets could not be verified from the primary docs. It remains a cheap one-week pilot before committing to the build.
