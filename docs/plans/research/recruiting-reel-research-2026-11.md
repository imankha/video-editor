# Recruiting reel research (T16320)

Written 2026-11-13. Status: findings complete. The owner ruled the same day: the design is general first with strings stored for each sport, Brilliant has one bar (section 8a), and every other recommendation in section 8 is accepted as written.

## How to read this

A reel is several highlights (finished short clips of an athlete) joined into one video for a college coach. This document answers the questions in the task file, proposes the list of qualities a soccer play can demonstrate, describes what makes a moment worth posting on social media, and ends with one-line recommendations to the tasks that depend on it.

Every answer carries an evidence strength:

- **Agreed**: many independent sources say the same thing.
- **A few**: two to four sources say it and none contradicts it.
- **Disputed**: sources contradict each other.
- **No evidence**: nobody addresses it; the recommendation is our judgment.

Three limits apply to everything below.

1. **Most advice comes from recruiting services, not coaches.** NCSA, SportsRecruits, Hudl, Trace, Veo and FieldLevel sell recruiting products and repeat each other. Named college coaches speaking for themselves are few: Mike Giuliano (Georgia Gwinnett women's soccer), Bryan Amos (former Duke assistant), Steve Coxon (former Division I recruiter), Ryan Gunderson (Nebraska football), five coaches quoted by SoccerWire, and Duke's staff in a 2012 talk.
2. **The best data is a 2025 survey of 178 NCAA Division I and II soccer coaches** (Andrew et al., PLOS One). It measures what coaches value in a player, not how a reel should be built.
3. **Pages were read through a summarising tool.** Short quotes are near-verbatim. Check any quote against its page before it goes into copy a user will see.

Sources are numbered in section 9.

## 1. What coaches look for and what makes them stop

**They look for** (Agreed):

- Decisions and technique shown in real games, with enough of the play before and after to see the choice the player made [6][13][26].
- Skills that fit the position. Amos: the video should say "this is what I can do, these are the skills that I have, this is how I'm going to help your program" [29].
- Game footage over training, for field players [2][6].

**They stop for** (Agreed):

- A slow start. Giuliano: "Don't build to a climax. Lead with your best material." [9]
- Padding. "Padding your reel with average plays dilutes the impression your best clips make." [7]
- Not being able to find the player (section 5).
- Heavy editing: transitions, music, graphics [7][5][6].
- Shaky, distant or blurry footage [26].

**How long they give it** (Agreed that the opening decides it; the number is Disputed):

| Stated window | Who |
|---|---|
| 15 seconds | One blog [20] |
| 20 to 30 seconds | Coxon, former Division I recruiter [12] |
| 30 seconds | NCSA, Hudl, Trace and three others [6][14][10][28][22] |
| 30 to 60 seconds | Gunderson, Nebraska football [21] |
| 60 to 90 seconds | Giuliano, the only soccer head coach with a number [9] |

Giuliano also said his program received 2,000 reel links in a year [9]. SportsRecruits reports an average coach viewing time of 3 minutes 18 seconds on its platform, but we could not open the page and it gives no method, so it is not relied on here.

**What it means for us:** the first three to five highlights carry the reel. Everything after them is read only by a coach who is already interested.

**Evidence: Agreed** on 3 to 5 minutes as the ceiling. **A few** on the clip count of 15 to 25.

**What real reels do** (section 7): the 20 reels we looked at run from 2:06 to 8:39 with a median of 4:17. Seven of the 20 run past five minutes, against the advice.

## 3. What goes first

**Best first** is the single most agreed rule in the research: twelve sources, including Giuliano and Gunderson [3][5][6][7][9][10][12][14][21][25][26][28][30]. How many "best" clips open the reel varies between two and five [7][2][3][5]. Nobody recommends date order. Football coaches say outright not to go game by game [21].

**Grouping by skill** is recommended by a few: US Club Soccer, Hudl, College Soccer Insight, Veo [3][14][28][20b]. **Disputed** only in the sense that grouping alone would bury the best clips.

The two fit together and several sources describe exactly this: open with the best three to five highlights whatever their type, then group the rest.

**What real reels do:** 3 of 20 use named sections, and all three are goalkeepers or defenders (shot stopping, distribution, crosses; goals, long balls, defending). None uses a full-screen section card; they print the section name over the footage or list the sections with times on one card at the start.

## 4. Graduation year, position, title card, end card, contact details

### Graduation year

**Agreed:** on the title card [2][3][5][6][7][14][26][28]. **A few:** also in the video's title and file name, in the pattern "Name | Class of 2027 | Position" [1][28]. **No evidence** for keeping it on screen throughout. 17 of our 20 reels show it on a card and all 20 put it in the YouTube title.

### Position

**Agreed:** on the title card. **No evidence** from any soccer source on how to show more than one. Sources write "position(s)" without comment [26][28]. In our 20 reels, 7 list more than one position, from two up to four ("LW, RW, ST, CAM"); one separates "primary position" from "alternate position".

**How long:** 3 to 4 seconds (Eastside FC [25]), 5 seconds at most (one guide), 10 seconds (Veo's sample [20b]). Our reels hold their cards between 1 and 15 seconds with a median near 5. The reels with the longest introductions (10 to 15 seconds of photo slides, honors and animated logos) are doing what five sources warn against [7][20][11][24].

**Cold open:** 3 of 20 reels show a play first and the card second. Two of these come from the same paid editing service. No written source recommends it, though it follows from "lead with your best material".

### End card

Only 3 of 20 reels have an end card with information on it; one more ends on an animated logo. Sixteen simply stop on the last clip. The sources that discuss an end card all say the same thing: name and contact details, and the current coach's name and email [6][14][20][20b]. **Evidence: A few.**

### Contact details

| Position | Who |
|---|---|
| Start and end | NCSA, Veo, one blog [6][20b][20] |
| Start only | IMG, SoccerWire, US Club Soccer, Coxon [5][2][3][12] |
| Start, with the coach's contact on the closing slide | Hudl [14] |
| Not in the video at all; in the profile or the email | SportsRecruits [7][27] |

**Evidence: Disputed.** In our reels, 12 of 20 show no contact details, 7 show them at the start and 1 at the end only.

**Whose details:**

- **The athlete's own.** Coxon: "Coaches want to connect directly with recruits." [18] Giuliano lists "Letting parents act as your agent" as a top recruiting mistake [9]. All 8 reels with contact details give the athlete's email. None gives a parent's.
- **The club or school coach's**, as a reference: NCSA, Hudl and Veo recommend it [6][14][20b]; SportsRecruits says leave it out [27]. 5 of 20 reels include a coach's email.
- **A reason the coach's contact matters for younger athletes:** in Division I soccer a college coach may not communicate with an athlete or parent before June 15 after the sophomore year [17]. That source is from 2019; the current calendar should be checked before we state the rule anywhere in the app. Before that date a college coach who is interested goes through the club coach.

One thing no source discusses and we should weigh: a recruiting reel is a public video of a minor. A phone number or email on the first frame is also on the cover image that every platform shows.

## 5. Pointing out the athlete

**Agreed** by thirteen sources: mark the athlete [1][2][3][5][6][7][12][13][14][24][25][26][28]. A jersey number alone is not enough on wide footage [1][2].

- **How:** circle, arrow, spotlight or a shaded spot under the player. The two camera services (Trace, Veo) offer a ring that follows the player.
- **When:** before the action starts [2][3][21][24]. SoccerWire: "Hold each clip for about two seconds before the action begins and mark your player with a circle." [2]
- **How often:** every clip [1][7][3].
- **The one caution** comes from Gunderson: mark the player before the play, and do not interrupt the play itself, because a chopped clip hides athleticism [21]. A marker that follows the player avoids the problem.
- **Keep one style** through the reel [24][28].

**What real reels do:** 12 of 20 mark the athlete. Ten use a still circle, arrow or box at the start of the clip; two use a ring that follows the player. Of the 8 without a marker, 4 are goalkeepers, where the player is obvious.

## 8. Proposals

### 8a. What makes a play Brilliant, and the quality list (ruled 2026-11-13)

The owner ruled on 2026-11-13 that the design is general first: one set of rules written for any sport, and for each sport a small set of strings, stored with that sport beside its tags, that are added to the prompt for athletes of that sport. Everything in this section follows that shape. Soccer is the first sport with strings; a sport with none still works from the general part.

#### The one bar

A play is Brilliant when the player did better than a good player at that level would be expected to do. That is the whole test, for every sport. A play is judged against the level of the game being watched, not against professionals.

There are two ways to clear the bar, and a play can clear both:

- **For scouting:** the player shows both a strong decision and strong execution, and the play demonstrates at least one quality from the list below. A good decision carried out badly, or a fine piece of skill that was the wrong choice, is not Brilliant for scouting.
- **For social media:** the play is the kind players see in highlight videos of their favorite players. The sport's own string names what those look like.

The analysis also finds **learning opportunities**: plays where the player could have chosen or executed better. They are kept with their lesson, are never Brilliant, and are never offered to the reel builder.

#### General qualities (stored once, used by every sport)

A quality is what a play shows about the athlete. A tag is what happened. A scoutable play carries one to three qualities.

| Stored name | Meaning | Counts when you see | Routine, does not count |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separation | Beats an opponent with speed, quickness or jump. | The gap between the player and an opponent changes because of the player's speed, quickness or jump: the player pulls away from one, catches one who started ahead, or gets higher than one in a contested jump. | No opponent was close enough to compare against, or the opponent was not trying. |
| Strength | Wins or keeps possession through contact. | In body contact with an opponent, the player stays balanced and comes away in control. | No opponent touched them. |
| Composure | Keeps control while opponents close in. | Under close pressure from one or more opponents, the player keeps control and makes a clean next action instead of giving possession away. | Time and space, nobody near. |
| Vision | Finds a teammate or a space that takes opponents out of the play. | The player delivers to a teammate or into a space that was not the nearest option, and it leaves one or more opponents behind the play. | The nearest, obvious option. |
| Decision Making | Chooses the better option before the opponent can react. | Two or more options were visible and the player took the one that hurt the opponent most, or released before the nearest opponent could reach them. | Only one option was on. |
| Anticipation | Moves early and arrives before the opponent. | The player moves before the opponent acts and gets there first: cuts out a pass, steps in front, or covers for a beaten teammate. | The play came straight to them. |
| Work Rate | Out-works the players around them to win possession back. | While teammates and opponents nearby slow down or jog, the player keeps sprinting or makes repeated efforts off the play, and it wins possession back or stops an attack. | Jogging back into position with everyone else. |
| Getting Open | Gets free of a marker without possession. | A timed move without possession leaves the marker behind or on the wrong side, and a teammate finds the player. | Standing in space and receiving. |
| 1v1 Attacking | Beats a defender alone. | Facing a set defender, the player gets past alone and the move leads to a scoring chance or advances the play. | The defender was out of position or not engaged. |
| 1v1 Defending | Stops an attacker alone. | Isolated against an attacker, the player stops them cleanly without help. | Help was already there and the attacker had slowed. |

**Relative, and visible on video (owner ruling, 2026-11-13).** Coaches say "pace" [Q5][Q9], and this list first used that word. The owner ruled that pace cannot be determined from video and is not a word every sport uses. What video does show is one player against another: the athlete gets away from an opponent with speed or quickness, catches up to one who started ahead, or jumps higher than one. That is "separation", and it reads the same in any sport. Every quality in the table is held to the same standard: its sign describes what the player does relative to another player in the frame, and none depends on a distance, a speed, or what the player sees or feels. Under that standard Pace became Separation. Off-Ball Movement became Getting Open, because the old name assumed a ball. Vision lost "looks around first" and Composure lost "stays calm", since a wide sideline camera that cannot read a jersey number cannot show a glance or a mood; both are now judged by what happens to the opponents. Work Rate is judged against the players nearby instead of by the length of a sprint. Strength, Anticipation, Decision Making and the two 1v1 qualities already met the standard. The names coaches use were kept wherever the sign could be made relative; only the two names above changed. Soccer's wording below lost its yard counts for the same reason.

Separation overlaps on purpose with two others. A recovery run that catches an attacker shows Separation (the athletic output) and may also show Work Rate (the effort when others had stopped). A jump that beats an opponent shows Separation, and in soccer also Aerial Ability when the header itself is won and directed.

How the list was built: the terms recur across the U.S. Soccer "Key Qualities of a Player" [Q5], the survey of 178 NCAA coaches [Q9], a real college camp evaluation report [Q8], and coach interviews [Q1][Q10], and each is a word coaches in other team sports also use. A term stayed only if a person can see it in one play on wide sideline footage. Left out on purpose because a single play cannot show them: coachability, communication, leadership, endurance and response to a mistake. Coaches say they judge these from full games and in person [Q9][Q11][Q12].

"Decision Making" stays as a quality even though every scoutable play needs a good decision. It is given only when the choice itself is what stands out. A game is analyzed once and stored, so a quality left out of the first version cannot be recovered without paying to analyze the game again, while one that proves unreliable can be ignored later.

The "does not count" column has almost no direct support from coaches. Sources say only to choose plays under pressure and against strong opponents [Q5][Q8][Q11]. The routine examples are our reading of that, to be corrected after the first staging game.

#### Soccer's strings

**Soccer's own qualities**

| Stored name | Meaning | Counts when you see | Routine, does not count |
|---|---|---|---|
| First Touch | Receives so the next action is easy. | A driven or bouncing ball, or one received under pressure, is controlled in one touch that moves it away from the defender and into the next pass, shot or dribble. | A slow pass with nobody near; a touch that needs two more to tidy. |
| Passing Range | Hits long or firm passes accurately. | A long pass that clears one or more lines of opponents and reaches a teammate's feet or stride; or a firm ground pass that goes between two defenders to a teammate. | Short sideways or backward passes under no pressure. |
| Finishing | Scores or tests the goalkeeper with technique. | A shot placed in a corner or past a set goalkeeper, a volley or header on target, a calm finish one against one, or a goal from outside the penalty area. | A tap-in to an empty net; a shot straight at the goalkeeper. |
| Delivery | Puts crosses and set pieces where teammates can score. | A cross, corner or free kick that reaches a teammate in the penalty area and leads to a shot or header. | Cleared by the first defender, or out of play. |
| Aerial Ability | Wins the ball in the air. | The player jumps against an opponent and wins the header, to a teammate, clear of danger or on target. | An unchallenged header. |
| Shot Stopping (goalkeeper) | Keeps out shots that should score. | A dive at full stretch, a reaction save from close range, a save one against one, or a second save straight after the first. | A slow shot hit straight at them. |
| Command of the Area (goalkeeper) | Controls the penalty area and the space behind the defense. | The goalkeeper comes off the line to catch or punch a cross among players, or leaves the area early to clear a through ball. | Picking up a ball nobody is chasing. |
| Distribution (goalkeeper) | Starts attacks with feet and hands. | A throw, kick or pass that reaches a teammate in stride beyond the first line of opponents; a calm pass out under pressure. | A long kick to nobody; a short roll to an unmarked defender. |

**The general qualities in soccer's words**

| General quality | In soccer |
|---|---|
| Separation | Runs away from a defender with or without the ball, catches an attacker who had a head start, or out-jumps an opponent for a header. |
| Strength | Shoulder to shoulder, stays up and comes away with the ball; or holds a defender off with the back to goal and plays a teammate in. |
| Composure | With a defender within reach, keeps the ball and passes or carries out instead of clearing it. |
| Vision | Plays through or over a line of defenders into a teammate's run, or switches play to the free side. |
| Decision Making | Squares to an unmarked teammate instead of shooting from a tight angle; releases in one or two touches before the press arrives. |
| Anticipation | Steps in to intercept the pass, or covers the space behind a beaten teammate before the attacker gets there. |
| Work Rate | After the ball is lost, sprints past jogging players to win it back or catch the attack; presses two or three times in a row and forces a turnover. |
| Getting Open | A run behind the last defender that stays onside and is found; an overlapping run that receives the pass. |
| 1v1 Attacking | Goes past a set defender with a change of speed or direction or a feint, and it leads to a shot, cross or pass. |
| 1v1 Defending | Stays on their feet, forces the attacker wide or backward, and wins the ball or blocks the cross or shot. |

**Soccer's social examples:** a goal from long range, a volley, an overhead or scissor kick, a diving header, a free kick into the top corner, a chip over the goalkeeper, the ball played through a defender's legs, a turn or skill move that leaves a defender behind, a run past several opponents, a full-stretch or double save, a penalty save, a clearance off the goal line, a goalkeeper scoring.

**Soccer's reel notes:** see section 8c.

### 8b. The evidence behind the social-media side

The owner's ruling replaces the six signs this research first proposed with one sentence: a play that clears the bar and is the kind players see in highlight videos of their favorite players. That sentence works for every sport, and each sport's social examples make it concrete.

The research supports the ruling as far as research exists, which is not far. No study measures which youth sports clips are shared. No platform publishes sport-specific guidance. The people who pick ESPN's daily top ten say they have no fixed criteria [S10]. What exists:

- Berger and Milkman (2012) found that content which produces awe or high arousal is shared more, and that surprising content is shared more. Their data was about 7,000 newspaper articles, not sport and not video [S1][S2].
- Reported youth and amateur soccer clips with stated reach, every one of them a play a professional highlight video would also show: a 15-year-old's diving header at a national championship [S3]; a high-school player's flip goal, a six-second clip "looped millions of times" [S6]; an under-12 goalkeeper scoring a last-minute winner from his own half, "viewed more than one million times" [S5]; a lob over a goalkeeper off his line, over 900,000 views [S4].

Two things the research found that the ruling settles by its wording: a child's mistake can spread widely but is not better than expected, so it is never Brilliant; and a moment of sportsmanship can spread but is not a play.

**Where the two kinds differ**, in soccer's terms:

| Play | Coach | Stranger |
|---|---|---|
| A calm pass out of pressure from the back | Values it: Composure | Scrolls past |
| An interception from reading the pass early | Values it: Anticipation | Scrolls past |
| A recovery run that catches the attacker, and a clean tackle | Values it: Separation, Work Rate, 1v1 Defending | Scrolls past |
| A trick that beats nobody and leads to nothing | Discounts it: "not just flashy tactics" [6] | May stop |
| A volley into the top corner from the edge of the area | Finishing | Stops |
| A dribble past two defenders and a finish | 1v1 Attacking, Finishing | Stops |

The claim that strangers scroll past a composed pass is our inference; no source tests it. The coach's side of the table is supported [6][7][26].

**For the short social cut** (not part of the recruiting reel): Meta's own guidance is portrait 9:16 with "a strong hook or payoff" [S13]; watching a clip to the end is a strong signal on TikTok [S9]. Advice about a hook in the first one to three seconds, captions and loops is repeated everywhere in marketing writing with no traceable data.

### 8c. Differences by sport

The same in nearly every sport: best first; keep it to about 2 to 5 minutes; a stable, wide, unobstructed camera; mark the athlete; name, year and position on a card; no music, effects or slow motion; a full game ready on request.

| Sport | What differs |
|---|---|
| Soccer | Field players: game play only, 3 to 6 minutes, 20 to 25 clips. Goalkeepers may add training footage for skills a game did not show [6]. |
| Basketball | Under 4 minutes, 20 to 30 clips from two or three games. Camera at mid-court. Send one unedited full game with the reel [B4][B5]. |
| Football | Best 25 plays. Height and weight on the card. Mark the athlete before every snap. Kickers, punters and long snappers use skills footage [B7][B8]. |
| Flag football | Almost nothing written. Five college coaches on one podcast: 30 seconds to impress, circle yourself, show flag pulls and catches against good opponents [B11]. |
| Baseball | A skills video, not game highlights: set numbers of swings, pitches and throws filmed from set positions, with pitch speed and exit speed shown [B13][B14]. |
| Softball | The same: coaches "want to see skills videos over highlight videos", 2 to 3 minutes [B16]. |
| Ice hockey | 3 to 4 minutes, 15 to 30 clips. Goalies add controlled drill footage. Measurements go in the email, not the reel [B17][B18]. |
| Lacrosse | Under 4 minutes, 20 to 30 clips. Goalies show saves against varied shots, clears and communication [B20][B21]. |
| Rugby | One real source. Match footage only, 7 to 10 minutes, plus a full match [B22]. |
| Volleyball | Skills by position mixed with game play. Camera behind the end line on the athlete's side. Height and vertical jump on the card [B25][B26]. |
| Tennis | Match points plus strokes. Camera high behind the court. Rating (UTR) on the card. Advice on length runs from 3 to 10 minutes [B28][B29]. |

A builder that joins game highlights fits soccer field players, basketball, football, flag football, lacrosse, hockey skaters and rugby. Baseball and softball want staged skills footage, which our highlights are not. Volleyball and tennis sit between.

### 8d. Recommendations to the tasks that depend on this

The owner accepted all of these on 2026-11-13. Each is written for any sport; where a sport differs, the difference lives in that sport's reel notes (section 8c).

**Title card and cover (T15880)**

1. Card holds name, graduation year, position, jersey number and club or team. Jersey number is on by default because coaches use it to find the player and most families forget it.
2. Position shows a primary position and up to two others.
3. Optional lines, off by default: high school, height, grade average.
4. No contact details on the title card by default. It is also the cover image shown publicly on every platform.
5. The reel's title and file name follow "Name | Class of 2027 | Position | Club".

**Cards and their lengths (T16300)**

6. Title card suggested at 3 to 4 seconds and section cards at 1.5 seconds; the planned words-based suggestion already lands there.
7. End card on by default for a recruiting reel, suggested at 5 seconds: name, graduation year, the athlete's email, and optionally the club coach's name and email. Phone number optional and off by default. No field for a parent.
8. A recruiting reel uses no slow motion, no music by default and plain cuts.

**The reel expert prompt (T16330)**

9. Open with the athlete's three to five strongest highlights whatever their type, then group the rest into two to four sections named for what this athlete has.
10. Aim for about 3 minutes and 12 to 20 highlights. Never suggest past 5 minutes. With few highlights, a shorter reel is better than a padded one.
11. Use only highlights from Brilliant plays and from plays the parent rated well; never a learning opportunity. Rank by what counts for scouting: plays under pressure and against a set opponent beat the same action unopposed. Do not pick a play only because it is Brilliant for social media.
12. Section names come from the athlete's sport's quality names, in the words coaches use. In soccer a goalkeeper's sections might be Shot Stopping, Distribution and Command of the Area.
13. Prefer landscape highlights. Prefer highlights where the athlete is pointed out. Prefer recent games.
14. Show range for the position: a reel should not be one kind of play repeated. In soccer, a center back's reel should not be only tackles and a goalkeeper's not only saves.
15. Baseball and softball run on the general part with a note that coaches in those sports expect a skills video.

**The game analysis (T16150)**

16. The prompt is built general first: the one bar and the general qualities in 8a, then the athlete's sport's strings. The sport's social examples describe the social kind. Learning opportunities are found and kept with their lesson, and are never Brilliant.
